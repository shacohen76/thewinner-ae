> **Quick Answer:** Start with 10 pages a day — not 30 minutes. Read what you enjoy, not what you think you should read. Keep a book visible where you usually scroll your phone. Habit beats ambition every time.

Building a reading habit feels impossible when you're staring at that stack of unfinished books on your nightstand. Trust me, I know exactly how that feels. Three years ago, I bought seven books during a particularly optimistic Dubai Mall visit, convinced that this time would be different. Those books sat there, collecting dust and silent judgment, until I finally admitted the truth: I was approaching reading completely wrong.

The problem isn't that we don't want to read — it's that we're trying to force reading into the wrong shape. We set impossible goals, pick books we think we should read instead of want to read, and then wonder why our motivation evaporates after three days.

Here's what I've learned after helping hundreds of people build sustainable reading habits: the secret isn't willpower or speed-reading techniques. It's designing a system so simple and enjoyable that not reading becomes harder than reading.

## Why Most Reading Habits Fail (And It's Not What You Think) {#why-most-reading-habits-fail}

Let me tell you about Ahmed, a software engineer I met at a BookFaire event in Abu Dhabi last year. He'd been trying to build a reading habit for five years — literally. Every January, he'd set the same goal: "Read 50 books this year." By March, he'd managed maybe three chapters across two different books, felt like a failure, and gave up until the next January.

Ahmed's problem wasn't lack of intelligence or interest. He was making the same three mistakes that derail 90% of reading attempts:

**Mistake #1: Setting time-based goals instead of page-based goals.** When you say "I'll read for 30 minutes," you're fighting the clock. Some days those 30 minutes feel like eternity because you're tired or the book is dense. Other days, you get absorbed and read for two hours, then feel like you "used up" your reading quota for the week.

**Mistake #2: Choosing books based on reputation rather than personal interest.** We pick classics we think we should read, business books our boss recommended, or bestsellers everyone's talking about. Then we wonder why we're not excited to open them.

**Mistake #3: Trying to read like we did in university.** You don't need to remember every detail, analyze themes, or feel guilty about skipping boring parts. Reading for pleasure is completely different from reading for exams.

![Infographic showing the three main reasons reading habits fail](/blog/images/guide-to-starting-reading-habit/why-habits-fail-infographic.jpg)

The neuroscience is clear: habits stick when they're easy, obvious, attractive, and satisfying. Most people make reading hard (impossible daily goals), hidden (no visual cues), unattractive (boring book choices), and unsatisfying (no sense of progress).

Here's what works instead: micro-habits that feel almost ridiculously easy, books you genuinely want to read, and a system that gives you small wins every day.

## The 10-Page Rule: Start Smaller Than You Think {#the-10-page-rule}

Forget everything you've heard about "reading for 30 minutes a day." Here's your new rule: 10 pages per day, no matter what.

I learned this from my friend Layla, a pediatric nurse who works 12-hour shifts at a hospital here in Dubai. She thought she'd never have time to read until she committed to exactly 10 pages every day. Not 10-15 pages. Not "around 10 pages." Exactly 10.

This sounds almost insultingly small, but it's genius for three reasons:

**It's genuinely achievable on your worst days.** Even when you're exhausted, sick, or dealing with a crisis, you can read 10 pages. It takes about 10-15 minutes for most people. You can do it during lunch, before bed, or while waiting for your coffee to brew.

**It creates momentum without overwhelm.** Ten pages is enough to maintain story flow and character connection, but not so much that it feels like work. You'll often find yourself reading more — but the minimum is always just 10.

**It compounds dramatically.** Ten pages daily equals 3,650 pages per year — roughly 12-15 average-length books. That's more than most people read in three years.

![Photo of someone reading exactly 10 pages marked with a bookmark](/blog/images/guide-to-starting-reading-habit/ten-pages-bookmark.jpg)

Here's how to implement the 10-page rule:

**Use physical page markers.** Whether you're reading physical books or using an [ebook reader](/best/ebook-reader), mark exactly where page 10 is before you start reading. This removes the mental math and gives you a clear stopping point.

**Count by your format, not "standard" pages.** If you're reading on your phone, 10 phone screens might equal 5 traditional pages — that's fine. The goal is consistency, not perfect measurement.

**Never read less, but always allow yourself to read more.** Some days you'll hit page 10 and want to keep going. That's fantastic. But never negotiate down from 10 — that breaks the habit.

After three months of the 10-page rule, Layla had finished four books — more than she'd read in the previous two years combined. The habit had become so automatic that she often forgot to count and just read until natural stopping points.

## Choosing Books That Actually Hook You {#choosing-books-that-hook-you}

This might be the most important section in this entire guide. You cannot build a reading habit with boring books, no matter how acclaimed they are.

I spent my twenties forcing myself through "important" literature because I thought that's what smart people read. I slogged through Tolstoy, struggled with Joyce, and felt guilty when I abandoned Infinite Jest on page 127. It wasn't until I gave myself permission to read thriller novels and sci-fi that I remembered why I loved reading in the first place.

**Start with genres you actually enjoy.** If you love crime shows, try mystery novels. If you're into technology, pick up science fiction or tech memoirs. If you enjoy celebrity gossip, read biographies. There's absolutely nothing wrong with "commercial" or "genre" fiction — many of [the best-selling books of all time](/blog/guide-to-best-selling-books-all-time) are genre novels that millions of people found impossible to put down.

**The 50-page test is your friend.** Give every book 50 pages to hook you. If you're not engaged by then, put it down without guilt. Life's too short for books that feel like homework.

**Ignore "should" entirely.** Don't read books because they won awards, because your book club chose them, or because they're classics you "should" have read by now. Read books because you're curious about what happens next.

![Photo of diverse book genres laid out on a table](/blog/images/guide-to-starting-reading-habit/diverse-genres.jpg)

Here's my personal system for [picking your next book](/blog/how-to-pick-your-next-book) when you're building a new habit:

**Start with authors who are natural storytellers.** Some writers prioritize prose style or literary themes; others are primarily concerned with keeping you turning pages. For habit-building, choose the page-turners. You can expand to more challenging material once the habit is solid.

**Look for books described as "addictive," "unputdownable," or "page-turner."** These aren't shameful qualities — they're exactly what you want when building a reading habit.

**Consider shorter books initially.** A 250-page thriller will give you the satisfaction of finishing something, which builds momentum for longer books. You can always graduate to 500-page epics later.

**Sample extensively before committing.** Amazon.ae lets you read the first few chapters of most ebooks. Use this feature liberally. If the opening doesn't grab you, try something else.

Remember: you're not building a reading habit to impress anyone or check boxes on some cultural literacy list. You're doing it because reading is one of life's great pleasures, and you deserve that pleasure.

## Creating Physical and Digital Reading Environments {#creating-reading-environments}

Your environment shapes your behavior more than your willpower does. If you want to read more, you need to design spaces that encourage reading and remove friction from the process.

**The visibility principle:** Keep a book visible where you usually mindlessly scroll your phone. I keep mine on the kitchen counter next to where I eat breakfast. My colleague Sarah keeps hers on the bathroom counter (don't judge — it works). The goal is to create visual cues that remind you of your reading intention.

**Multiple access points:** Don't rely on having your book with you at all times. Keep a physical book at home, another in your car, and have the ebook version on your phone. This eliminates the "I forgot my book" excuse.

**Lighting matters more than you think.** Poor lighting is the fastest way to make reading feel like work. Invest in a good reading light for your favorite chair — something with adjustable brightness and color temperature. Many people in the UAE prefer cooler light in the morning and warmer light in the evening.

![Photo of a cozy reading nook with good lighting and accessible books](/blog/images/guide-to-starting-reading-habit/reading-environment.jpg)

**The phone distance rule:** Keep your phone at least arm's length away while reading. This is crucial. When your phone is within easy reach, you'll check it constantly without realizing it, breaking your reading flow.

**Create a "reading kit" for different scenarios:**

- **Bedside:** Physical book, bookmark, small reading light, glass of water
- **Living room:** Comfortable cushion, side table for coffee, good overhead lighting
- **Commute:** Phone with ebook app, comfortable earbuds if you prefer audiobooks
- **Work:** Digital book on your computer for lunch break reading

**Temperature and comfort:** This sounds basic, but reading requires sustained focus, which is impossible if you're too hot, too cold, or sitting uncomfortably. Have a light blanket nearby, ensure good air circulation, and choose seating that supports reading for 20-30 minutes without adjustment.

I learned this lesson the hard way during my first attempt at building a reading habit. I tried to read in bed using only the overhead light, propped up against flat pillows, with my phone charging right next to me. I lasted about four days before my neck hurt, my eyes were strained, and I was checking Instagram every two minutes.

## The Technology Question: E-readers vs Physical Books {#technology-question}

The great format debate rages on, but here's the truth: the best reading format is whichever one you'll actually use consistently. I've seen people build strong habits with physical books, e-readers, tablets, and even phones. The format matters less than the consistency.

That said, each format has distinct advantages for habit building:

**Physical books excel at:**
- No battery anxiety or technical issues
- Natural progress tracking (you can see how much you've read)
- No digital distractions
- Better retention for some people (the physical memory of turning pages)
- Work in direct sunlight and anywhere else

**E-readers are superior for:**
- Carrying multiple books without weight
- Reading in dark environments with built-in lighting
- Adjustable text size for comfort
- Instant access to new books
- Travel and commuting

![Comparison chart showing pros and cons of different reading formats](/blog/images/guide-to-starting-reading-habit/format-comparison.jpg)

If you're choosing an [ebook reader](/best/ebook-reader), prioritize battery life and eye comfort over fancy features. The [Amazon](https://amazon.com) Kindle ecosystem offers the largest selection of English books available in the UAE, while [Kobo](https://www.kobo.com) readers often support more file formats if you prefer flexibility.

**My personal recommendation:** Start with whichever format you already own. Don't let equipment shopping delay your habit building. I've seen too many people spend weeks researching the "perfect" e-reader while never actually reading anything.

If you do want to invest in dedicated reading technology, consider this progression:
1. **Start with your phone** using apps like Kindle, Apple Books, or Kobo
2. **If you enjoy digital reading**, upgrade to a dedicated e-reader for better eye comfort
3. **If you prefer physical books**, focus your budget on better lighting and comfortable seating

The key insight: format preferences often change as your habit develops. I started with physical books, switched to e-readers for convenience, and now use both depending on the situation. Don't feel locked into any single choice.

## Building Reading Into Your Existing Routines {#building-into-routines}

The most successful reading habits piggyback on routines you already have. This is called "habit stacking" — you attach your new habit to an established behavior.

**Morning routine stacking:** If you drink coffee every morning, read your 10 pages while the coffee is brewing or during your first cup. The coffee becomes your cue, and finishing your pages before starting work gives you an early sense of accomplishment.

**Commute reading:** For those using Dubai Metro or bus systems, commuting is perfect reading time. The fixed schedule creates natural boundaries, and you're already in a passive state where reading feels natural.

**Bedtime replacement:** Instead of scrolling your phone before sleep (which disrupts your circadian rhythm), read for 15-20 minutes. This serves double duty: building your reading habit and improving your sleep quality.

**Lunch break enhancement:** If you usually eat lunch alone, bring a book instead of defaulting to your phone. Many people find this transforms their lunch break from mindless scrolling into the day's most rejuvenating period.

![Diagram showing how to stack reading habits onto existing routines](/blog/images/guide-to-starting-reading-habit/habit-stacking-diagram.jpg)

**The two-minute rule:** On days when your routine is disrupted, commit to reading for just two minutes. This maintains the habit chain even when you can't do your full 10 pages. Two minutes is better than zero minutes.

**Weekend routine adjustment:** Weekends often derail weekday habits because your routine changes completely. Plan for this by identifying a weekend-specific reading time — perhaps Saturday morning with breakfast or Sunday afternoon during your usual TV time.

Here's what worked for me: I stacked reading onto my existing morning routine right after I pour my first cup of coffee. The coffee smell became my reading cue, and I trained myself not to check email or news until after my 10 pages. Within six weeks, reaching for my book became as automatic as reaching for my coffee.

**Dealing with routine disruptions:** Travel, illness, and schedule changes will happen. The key is having backup micro-routines. Keep a book in your car for unexpected waiting time. Have the ebook app on your phone for situations where you forgot your physical book. Build flexibility into your system from the start.

## Handling the Inevitable Obstacles {#handling-obstacles}

Every reading habit faces predictable challenges. The difference between people who succeed and those who quit is preparation for these obstacles.

**Obstacle 1: "I don't have time"**
This is rarely about actual time scarcity — it's about perceived time scarcity. Track how you spend time for three days. Include phone usage (most phones track this automatically). You'll discover pockets of time you didn't realize existed.

Most people find 20-30 minutes of "dead time" daily: waiting in line, early for appointments, during commercial breaks, before falling asleep. The 10-page rule turns this scattered time into reading time.

**Obstacle 2: "I keep forgetting"**
This is an environmental design problem, not a memory problem. Your cues aren't strong enough. Place your book somewhere you'll definitely see it. Set a phone reminder for your chosen reading time. Use habit tracking apps if that motivates you.

**Obstacle 3: "I can't focus/keep getting distracted"**
Modern brains are trained for constant stimulation. Reading requires sustained attention, which feels uncomfortable initially. This improves with practice, but you can help the process:
- Start with highly engaging books
- Read in 10-minute chunks if 20 minutes feels too long
- Eliminate obvious distractions (phone, TV, notifications)
- Try background instrumental music if complete silence feels wrong

![Photo of someone reading while managing common distractions](/blog/images/guide-to-starting-reading-habit/managing-distractions.jpg)

**Obstacle 4: "This book is boring, but I should finish it"**
Permission to quit boring books is essential for habit building. The goal is to become a reader, not to finish specific books. Keep a "did not finish" list without guilt. Successful readers abandon more books than unsuccessful readers.

**Obstacle 5: "I missed a day and ruined my streak"**
Perfectionist thinking kills habits. Missing one day doesn't erase weeks of progress. The rule is simple: never miss twice in a row. If you miss Monday, Tuesday becomes critical. If you miss Monday and Tuesday, Wednesday becomes absolutely essential.

**Obstacle 6: "Reading feels slow compared to audiobooks/videos"**
Reading is slower than most modern media consumption, but that's a feature, not a bug. The deliberate pace allows for deeper processing and retention. If speed is genuinely important, you can gradually increase your reading pace, but don't sacrifice comprehension for speed.

**The plateau problem:** After 2-3 months, your progress might feel less noticeable. You're reading regularly, but it doesn't feel exciting anymore. This is normal and expected. Push through this phase by varying your book selection, trying new genres, or setting small challenges (like reading a book by an author from a country you've never visited).

Remember that building a reading habit is like starting any new hobby: there's an initial learning curve, then a period of steady improvement, followed by plateaus and breakthroughs. Trust the process and focus on consistency over perfection.

## Tracking Progress Without Becoming Obsessive {#tracking-progress}

Tracking your reading progress can be motivating, but it can also become a source of stress if you're not careful. The goal is to maintain momentum and celebrate small wins, not to turn reading into a competitive sport.

**Simple tracking that works:**
- **Visual progress:** Use bookmarks to mark your daily 10-page goal. Seeing the bookmark move through the book provides tangible progress feedback.
- **Book completion list:** Simply write down each book you finish with the date. This creates a satisfying record of achievement without daily pressure.
- **Habit chain calendar:** Mark an X on your calendar for each day you read your minimum pages. Focus on not breaking the chain, but don't restart from zero if you miss a day.

**Apps and digital tools:**
Goodreads remains the most popular reading tracker, allowing you to set annual goals, rate books, and see what friends are reading. However, be careful with reading challenges — they can shift your focus from enjoyment to book completion quotas.

![Screenshot showing a simple reading progress tracking method](/blog/images/guide-to-starting-reading-habit/progress-tracking.jpg)

**Red flags in tracking:**
- Feeling guilty when you don't hit arbitrary daily targets
- Choosing shorter books just to increase your "books completed" number
- Comparing your reading speed or volume to others
- Abandoning good books because they're "taking too long"

**Advanced tracking for serious readers:**
Once your habit is solid (6+ months of consistent reading), you might enjoy tracking:
- Genres you gravitate toward
- Authors you want to explore more
- Books that significantly influenced your thinking
- Re-read books and when you want to revisit them

The most important metric is the simplest: are you reading more this month than you were six months ago? If yes, your system is working regardless of specific page counts or completion rates.

## Advanced Strategies for Committed Readers {#advanced-strategies}

Once you've maintained a consistent reading habit for several months, you can experiment with techniques that enhance your reading experience and help you get more value from books.

**The multiple book strategy:** Instead of reading one book at a time, keep 2-3 books in rotation: a fiction book for entertainment, a non-fiction book for learning, and perhaps an audiobook for commuting. This prevents you from getting stuck when one book loses momentum.

**Genre cycling:** Plan your reading across different genres to maintain interest and develop diverse perspectives. Follow a thriller with a biography, then a science book, then literary fiction. This variety keeps your reading experience fresh and prevents mental fatigue from similar writing styles.

**The commonplace book method:** Keep a notebook specifically for capturing interesting quotes, ideas, and reflections from your reading. This isn't about formal book reviews — it's about preserving thoughts that resonate with you. Many successful readers in the UAE find this practice transforms reading from passive consumption to active engagement.

![Photo of a reading journal with notes and quotes from books](/blog/images/guide-to-starting-reading-habit/reading-journal.jpg)

**Strategic re-reading:** Some books reveal new layers upon re-reading, especially complex fiction or philosophy books. Keep a "re-read someday" list of books that made significant impressions. You'll often discover that books you loved at 25 speak to you differently at 35.

**Building reading communities:** Once you're reading consistently, consider joining or creating reading communities. This might mean local book clubs, online discussion groups, or simply talking about books with friends and family. Social engagement with reading material increases retention and enjoyment.

**Seasonal and themed reading:** Some readers enjoy seasonal themes — horror novels in October, travel books before vacations, or business books at the start of the year. This creates anticipation and gives structure to your reading choices.

**The "book sabbatical" concept:** Occasionally take breaks from your usual genres to explore completely different types of writing. Spend a month reading only poetry, graphic novels, or books originally written in languages other than English. These experiments often reveal new interests and expand your definition of what counts as "good" reading.

Similar to [starting any new hobby](/blog/how-to-start-a-new-hobby), reading becomes more rewarding as you develop sophistication and personal preferences. The key is to let your reading habit evolve naturally rather than forcing it into rigid structures.

## Common Mistakes That Sabotage Reading Habits {#common-mistakes}

After years of helping people build reading habits, I've noticed the same mistakes repeatedly derail otherwise promising starts. Here are the most common pitfalls and how to avoid them:

**Mistake 1: Setting goals based on other people's reading speeds**
You see someone posting about reading 100 books per year and think you should match that pace. This is like trying to run a marathon because your neighbor runs marathons — completely irrelevant to your current fitness level and goals.

**Solution:** Focus only on reading more than your previous self. If you read 3 books last year, aim for 5-6 this year. Progress is personal.

**Mistake 2: Buying too many books at once**
Book buying can become a substitute for book reading. You feel productive purchasing books, but then feel overwhelmed by your growing "to-read" pile.

**Solution:** Follow the "one book ahead" rule — never own more than one unread book at a time until your habit is solid.

**Mistake 3: Forcing yourself through books you dislike**
Many people treat every book like a commitment that must be honored. This turns reading into a chore rather than a pleasure.

**Solution:** Implement the 50-page rule ruthlessly. Life is too short for books that don't engage you after giving them a fair chance.

![Infographic showing the most common reading habit mistakes](/blog/images/guide-to-starting-reading-habit/common-mistakes.jpg)

**Mistake 4: Reading only "improving" books**
Some new readers think every book must teach them something practical or make them smarter. This eliminates the simple pleasure of storytelling and entertainment.

**Solution:** Include fiction, humor, memoirs, and other "just for fun" books in your rotation. Entertainment has value too.

**Mistake 5: Neglecting physical comfort**
Trying to read in poor lighting, uncomfortable positions, or distracting environments makes reading feel like work.

**Solution:** Invest in your reading environment the same way you'd invest in a home office or workout space.

**Mistake 6: All-or-nothing thinking**
Missing one day becomes "I've ruined my streak" which becomes abandoning the habit entirely.

**Solution:** Never miss twice in a row, but don't restart your mental counter every time you miss once.

**Mistake 7: Comparing reading to other media consumption**
"I could watch three episodes in the time it takes me to read this chapter" misses the point entirely.

**Solution:** Understand that reading offers different benefits than passive media consumption — deeper focus, imagination engagement, and cognitive development.

These mistakes are normal parts of developing any new habit. The key is recognizing them quickly and adjusting your approach rather than abandoning the effort entirely.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q: How long does it take to build a solid reading habit?

Research suggests that simple habits take 21-66 days to become automatic, but reading habits often take longer because they require sustained mental effort. In my experience, most people feel like reading is becoming natural after about 6-8 weeks of consistent daily practice. The key milestone is when you start reaching for your book automatically during downtime rather than your phone. Don't worry if it takes longer — focus on consistency rather than speed of habit formation.

### Q: Should I set a yearly reading goal like "50 books per year"?

Yearly reading goals can be motivating for some people but problematic for others. They often lead to choosing shorter books, abandoning longer books you're enjoying, or feeling guilty when life circumstances change your reading time. Instead, focus on process goals like "read 10 pages every day" or "finish one book per month." These are more controllable and sustainable. If you do set numerical goals, base them on your current reading level, not aspirational numbers you see online.

### Q: Is it okay to read multiple books at the same time?

Absolutely, and many successful readers prefer this approach. Reading multiple books prevents you from getting stuck when one book loses momentum, and allows you to match your reading choice to your mood and energy level. A common strategy is keeping three books in rotation: one fiction for entertainment, one non-fiction for learning, and perhaps an audiobook for commuting. Just ensure you're making progress on at least one book rather than starting many and finishing none.

### Q: What should I do if I have trouble concentrating while reading?

Difficulty concentrating on reading is increasingly common in our digital age. Start with highly engaging, fast-paced books rather than dense or literary material. Break reading into shorter sessions — even 5-10 minutes is better than nothing. Eliminate distractions by keeping your phone in another room and reading in a quiet space. Consider instrumental background music if complete silence feels uncomfortable. Your concentration will improve with practice, but it takes time to retrain your brain for sustained focus.

### Q: How do I choose between physical books, e-readers, and audiobooks?

The best format is whichever one you'll use consistently. Physical books offer tactile satisfaction and no battery concerns. E-readers provide convenience, adjustable text, and built-in lighting. Audiobooks work well for commuting and multitasking, though some people retain less information from audio format. Many successful readers use different formats for different situations — physical books at home, e-books for travel, audiobooks while exercising. Don't feel pressured to choose just one format.

### Q: Is it normal to forget details from books I've read?

Yes, completely normal. Even voracious readers forget most details from books they've read. The value of reading isn't perfect retention — it's the cumulative effect of engaging with different ideas, perspectives, and ways of thinking. If retention is important to you, try taking brief notes while reading, discussing books with others, or keeping a simple journal of interesting quotes or ideas. But don't let imperfect memory discourage your reading habit.
